## FAQ: common questions {#faq}

### Ingredients and storage {#faq-ingredients}

::: q #faq-yeast-types What is the difference between dry and fresh yeast?
**Fresh yeast** (compressed, in a block) is a moist mass of living yeast cells. It has a short shelf life and has to be kept in the fridge. Before use, crumble it into the water and rub it until dissolved.

**Dry yeast** is dehydrated yeast with a much longer shelf life. It comes in two kinds: **instant**, which you can add straight to the flour, and **active dry**, which is usually dissolved beforehand in a small amount of lukewarm water – part of the water in the recipe; measure the rest cold.

**Conversion:** instant yeast is about 1/3 of the weight of fresh yeast (1 g of fresh ≈ 0.33 g of instant), and active dry about 0.4 of that weight (1 g of fresh ≈ 0.4 g of active dry). Manufacturers give different values, so check the recommendations on the packaging.

Fresh yeast is still the best choice: it is inexpensive and makes it easier to calculate the amount you need. After opening a jar or a larger pack of dry yeast, close it tightly, keep it in the fridge and use it within the time given by the manufacturer (usually within 2–3 months). It is best to use up a sachet at once.
:::

::: q #faq-yeast-no-scale How do I measure the yeast if I have no jewellery scale?
An ordinary kitchen scale (accurate to 1 g) cannot measure the 1.24 g of yeast in the basic recipe. Make a yeast solution of known concentration:

1. Dissolve 10 g of fresh yeast in 490 g of cold water and stir well: the yeast makes up 2% of the solution.
2. Take 50 g of solution for every 1 g of yeast in the recipe, that is 62 g for 1.24 g, and add 268 g of cold water instead of 329 g.
3. Do not keep the leftover solution.

**Instant yeast.** Dissolve a whole sachet of known weight (for example 7 g) in 49 times its weight of room-temperature water (343 g); the basic recipe needs about 20 g of this solution, and you add that much less water.

This method is less accurate than a jewellery scale, but it is good enough for your first bakes. The conversion for dry yeast is in the question about the [difference between dry and fresh yeast](#faq-yeast-types).
:::

::: q #faq-freeze-yeast Can fresh yeast be frozen?
It can, but after freezing the yeast usually loses its structure and becomes softer, almost liquid. Its effectiveness also drops: some of the yeast “dies off” and stops working, so it is worth increasing the amount. That is why fresh, unfrozen yeast is the better choice: it is inexpensive and makes it easier to calculate the amount you need. Stored properly, in an airtight box in the fridge, yeast usually keeps its strength for 1–4 weeks, depending on the maker (go by the date on the pack); after that it weakens and the dough may not rise.
:::

::: q #faq-freeze-mozzarella Can mozzarella balls be frozen?
You can freeze mozzarella in its original packaging (with whey or water), or just the balls sealed in airtight bags. After thawing, the cheese becomes “looser” and may fall apart more, because ice crystals affect its structure. Thaw it in the fridge, drain the water that comes out ([draining](#faq-drain-mozzarella)) and use it baked on pizza, not raw.
:::

::: q #faq-leftovers How do I store and reheat leftover pizza?
Put leftover pizza into an airtight container in the fridge within 2 hours of baking and eat it within 3–4 days. Reheat slices in a hot oven (about 200°C) or in a frying pan until they are steaming hot all the way through (74°C in the middle).
:::

::: q #faq-drain-mozzarella Should mozzarella balls be drained?
Yes, if the cheese is watery (for example mozzarella in brine or buffalo mozzarella): cut or tear it into pieces and leave it in a sieve in the fridge for a few hours. You will find more about cheeses in the [Mozzarella](#mozzarella) section.
:::

::: q #faq-tomato-sauce How do I prepare tomato sauce for pizza and store it?
A good pizza sauce is just tomatoes and salt.

1. Use peeled tomatoes (pelati) or finely chopped ones (polpa di pomodoro).
2. For every 100 g of unsalted tomatoes, add 1 g of salt (if the tomatoes are already salted, check the label and add less).
3. Crush the tomatoes by hand, or pulse them briefly in a blender so that the sauce keeps small pieces.

**Do not cook, reduce or pasteurise the sauce, and do not add sugar to it.** It finishes cooking on the pizza. The sweetness of the sauce depends on the brand and variety of the tomatoes.

**Storage:** do not keep the sauce in an open tin. Transfer it to an airtight container (glass or plastic), store it in the fridge and use it within 3–4 days. You can freeze a larger amount in portions.
:::

::: q #faq-starter-flours Which flours should I start with, and how do they differ?
The basic recipe is written for pizza flour of medium strength (W250–310). If the pack does not show W, go by the protein in the nutrition table. Such flours are forgiving of mistakes and let you bake pizza even in a home oven, which is why they work best to start with. They differ mainly in strength (W), protein content and the hydration they handle in practice:

| Flour | Strength W | Protein | Hydration in practice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Polselli Classica (blue) | 270 | about 11.5–12% | 60–63% |
| Polselli Vivace (red) | 290 | 13% | 60–65% |
| Caputo Pizzeria | 260–280 | 12.5% | – |
| Pasini Verde | 280–310 | at least 12.5% | 56–58% |
| Molino Spadoni PZ3 | about 280 | about 12.5–13% | – |
| 5 Stagioni Napoletana | about 300 | 13% | about 58% to start |

The higher the strength and protein content, the more water the flour absorbs and the longer the dough can mature (see [flour strength](#flour-strength)). Medium-strength flours usually have about 11.5–13 g of protein per 100 g. The W and protein values come from the technical data sheets of manufacturers or sellers and may differ between batches, and the hydrations in the last column come from home bakers’ experience – to start with, keep to a hydration of 55–58% (the basic recipe uses 55%). The full list is in the section [Pizza flours](#popular-flours).
:::

::: q #faq-black-dots What do the black dots on the dough mean?
They are usually harmless. Possible causes are:

- **bran particles** from the flour, which darken over time and are therefore more visible after a longer maturation (more often in less refined flours);
- **yeast that was not dissolved properly** – which is why you should crumble the yeast into the water and rub it until dissolved before adding it to the flour.

**Mould looks different.** It is fluffy or raised and damp, and green, blue, white, grey or black in colour, and the dough smells musty. If spots grow over time, smear or come with an unpleasant smell, throw the dough away (do not sniff it from close up).

Do not confuse these with the dark spots on the baked rim of the pizza, which are [leopard spotting](#faq-leopard). Black specks on the bottom are most often burnt dusting flour (see [burnt bottom](#faq-burnt-bottom)).
:::
