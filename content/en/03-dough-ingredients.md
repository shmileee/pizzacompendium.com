## Dough ingredients {#dough-ingredients}

**Flour** – the most important ingredient of the dough. Pizza is usually made with “00” flour (the most refined, with the most bran removed, and often also very finely milled). According to the specification (“disciplinare”) of the AVPN (Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana), Napoletana can also be made with type “0” (slightly less refined, with a little bran) and type “1” (as an admixture of 5–20%). For a better flavour, some bakers mix 00/0 flours with Tipo 1, Tipo 2 or wholemeal (integrale) flours.

Good pizza can also be made with ordinary wheat flours that are not meant for pizza, if they contain about 12% protein (check the pack), but the result is less predictable: stay at 55% hydration and judge the dough by how it looks, not only by the clock. You can also try simpler Italian pizza flours of lower strength, such as **Divella**. It is best, however, to choose professional flours, because they are usually more predictable (their parameters, such as the strength W, are given). You will find the individual flours described in the [list of flours](#popular-flours).

**Water** – the second most important ingredient after flour. If your tap water is clean and has no noticeable taste (for example of chlorine), you can use it; if it does, use bottled water. Always use cold water, and when the room temperature exceeds 25°C, you can use water straight from the fridge.

::: warning Cold water
**Do not use warm water.** If you use warm water, the dough will get too warm during kneading (it is usually accepted that it should not stay above about 25–26°C for long), which weakens its structure. In addition, the yeast gets going too early, and a dough that rises too fast has no time to mature ([about maturation](#rising-vs-maturation)).
:::

**Yeast** – it is best to use fresh (compressed) yeast, which is what the recipe in this guide is based on. Check the expiry date: yeast that is close to its expiry date may not “get going” the way it should.

As a fallback you can use dry yeast; you will find the conversion, the differences and how to dissolve active dry yeast (in lukewarm water) [in the answer on types of yeast](#faq-yeast-types). The advantage of dry yeast is its long shelf life, but once the pack has been opened it must be kept tightly closed in the fridge and used within the time given by the maker.

::: tip
**How to store fresh yeast?** It is best kept in the fridge in a small airtight container. It then does not dry out and lasts longer – usually 1–4 weeks (it gradually loses strength, so go by the date on the pack).
:::

**Salt** – use whatever you have on hand: sea, rock or Himalayan salt all work. Salt is essential, because it gives the dough strength and springiness during rising, and without it the pizza will not turn out well. Usually about 40–60 g is added per litre of water (for example, 50 g per litre of water is about 2.7% of the flour weight at 55% hydration).

**Fat** – prevents the dough from drying out and makes it tender after baking. It is usually added to pizzas baked below about 350°C; no fat goes into a Napoletana. You can use olive oil or another oil, and some bakers even use lard.

::: tip Good to know
**Which pizzas use fat?** Usually pizzas other than Napoletana (for example Classica, Tonda, Teglia and Pala alla Romana, and often Pinsa). A crisp crust is important in them, and they are usually baked at about 250–350°C.
:::

**Sugar** – Italian pizza dough does not need it.

::: warning No sugar
**Do not add sugar to the dough.** Flour already contains enough sugars for the yeast to work.
:::
