## Sauce, cheese and toppings {#toppings}

**Tomato sauce** – use peeled tomatoes (pelati) or finely chopped tomatoes (polpa di pomodoro). Add 1 g of salt per 100 g of unsalted tomatoes (if the tomatoes are already salted, check the label and add less). Crush the tomatoes by hand or blend them briefly in pulse mode – according to the traditional rule, longer blending breaks the seeds, which can make the sauce bitter. The sweetness of the sauce depends on the brand and variety of the tomatoes.

Well-known brands of Italian tomatoes include Mutti, Petti, Strianese and Rosso Gargano.

**Do not cook, reduce or pasteurise the sauce, and do not add sugar to it** – it finishes cooking on the pizza. Keep opened sauce in an airtight container in the fridge and use it within 3–4 days.

**How much?** For one pizza of 30–33 cm, about 60–80 g of sauce, 80–100 g of mozzarella, a few basil leaves and about 5–7 g (1–1.5 teaspoons) of olive oil are enough. For four pizzas that is roughly 240–320 g of sauce (one 400 g tin of tomatoes is enough) and 320–400 g of cheese. Do not overdo it: too many toppings release water.

**Cheese – mozzarella.** Use good-quality mozzarella, ideally fresh and Italian. The types are described below.

### Mozzarella {#mozzarella}

Common types of mozzarella include:

- mozzarella in brine (e.g. Galbani)
- mozzarella di bufala, made from buffalo milk (e.g. Valcolatte)
- mozzarella fior di latte, made from cow’s milk (e.g. Valcolatte or Gioiella Fior di Latte)
- shredded or strip (julienne) mozzarella
- block mozzarella

::: tip Good to know: cheese for pizza
The choice of cheese depends on what you want to achieve. For Napoletana, use mozzarella di bufala or fior di latte.

Always drain bufala, and mozzarella in brine is worth draining too. Pour off the brine, cut or tear the cheese into pieces and leave it on a sieve in the fridge for several hours; it is best to start when the dough balls are resting. Remember that cheese taken out of the fridge releases a bit more water as it warms up at room temperature, and bufala usually releases more than fior di latte.

You can control the structure of the cheese on the pizza with the size of the pieces, the temperature and the baking time. Bufala behaves differently after 90 seconds at 480°C than after 4–6 minutes at 250°C in an ordinary home oven. If your oven does not reach 450–500°C and you do not want a puddle on the pizza, choose a less watery cheese such as fior di latte or block mozzarella. Larger pieces of cheese usually help as well.

Cheese cut into julienne (strips of varying thickness) melts faster when the strips are thin and more slowly when they are thick. Dry mozzarella (block or shredded) releases much less water and bakes through more easily, but it makes a different kind of pizza.
:::

### Cured meats, vegetables and fruit {#other-toppings}

**Cured meats and other meats** – there is plenty of room to experiment here, but choose good-quality products, because poor ones can ruin the whole pizza.

**Vegetables and fruit** – the range of flavours is just as wide. Use those that contain a lot of water (e.g. tomatoes) sparingly: during baking the water runs out and the dough gets soggy.
