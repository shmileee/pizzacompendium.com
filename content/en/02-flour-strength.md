## Flour strength and protein {#flour-strength}

**What is flour strength?** The “W” value is the result of testing the dough with an alveograph (a laboratory instrument); you will come across it mostly on Italian flours, especially those meant for professional use. The higher the W, the more water the flour will usually absorb during kneading (with exceptions caused by differences in protein content) and the more elastic the dough will be, so it can rise and mature for longer.

Flour strength also depends on the protein content. If the pack shows no “W”, go by the protein in the nutrition table (g per 100 g): you can use it to estimate which flour to choose.

**Classification of pizza flours by strength and protein content.** Pizza flours are usually divided into three categories (a conventional split; other sources use other thresholds):

- **Low-strength flours, W220–240** (protein about 10.5–11.5%) – dough made from these flours usually matures in about **6–8 h** at room temperature, so they work best for quick pizzas. Suggested hydration depends on the protein content (the more protein, the higher): **54–58%**.
- **Medium-strength flours, W250–310** (protein about 11.5–13%) – dough made from these flours usually matures in about **8–16 h** at room temperature. They suit single-stage dough (diretto) well, and with a higher protein content you can also make a two-stage dough ([biga or poolish](#faq-biga-poolish)). They work well for **Napoletana** and **Classica** pizza. In home recipes these flours are most often used at a hydration of **58–65%**, depending on the protein content (beginners are better off starting at 55–58%).
- **High-strength flours, W320–400** (protein from about 13.5%) – dough made from these flours usually matures in about **14–20 h** at room temperature. They are often used for doughs with a hydration of 70% or more. With these flours you can comfortably make a two-stage dough ([biga or poolish](#faq-biga-poolish)). They are often chosen for Napoletana with a very long maturation, Canotto-style Napoletana, Teglia alla Romana, Pala alla Romana and similar high-hydration doughs.

Pizza flours start at about W220 (that is still low strength; medium strength is W250–310). There are also flours of lower strength and with a lower protein content (about 9–10%), but they are usually not suitable for pizza with a long maturation: they will only cope with a very short rise of a few hours. There are also flours with a strength above W400. They are usually meant for doughs that need a very long rise and maturation, such as panettone and colomba (Italian festive cakes). Some mills recommend them for pizza or high-hydration focaccia, but such experiments are best left to experienced bakers.

::: warning Treat these as a guide
The parameters above are a **reference point, not rigid limits**. Results may differ because of a different room temperature and the amount of yeast: the time ranges assume a room temperature of about 20–23°C. Also keep in mind that flour strength is not always directly proportional to the amount of protein: some flours have a high “W” value but less protein.
:::

*The comparison above is approximate and is based on values for **pizza flour** from the technical data sheets of selected Italian mills (Polselli, Caputo, Molino Pasini, Petra from Molino Quaglia, Molino Iaquone), where they give alveograph parameters (W, P/L).*
