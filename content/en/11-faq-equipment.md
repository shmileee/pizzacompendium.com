### Equipment, accessories and baking methods {#faq-equipment}

::: q #faq-stone Which stone should I choose for a home oven or a pizza oven, and how do I use it?
**Round or rectangular?** A rectangular stone is better, because it is easier to work with: there is a lower risk that a pizza launched onto it will land unevenly or slide off. Such stones are usually about 35×40 cm. When buying, check that the stone is suitable for food contact.

**What thickness?** Preferably from 1.5 to 3 cm. In a home oven a thickness of 1.5–2 cm works better, because the stone heats up faster. Stones 3 cm thick suit more powerful ovens; in ordinary ovens the heating time is too long. It is better to avoid stones about 1 cm thick, because they lose their temperature too quickly. You have to reheat them often and leave longer breaks between pizzas so that the stone can recover.

**How long should I heat the stone in the oven?** It depends on the maximum temperature of the oven, the position of the stone and its thickness. For example (approximate values, which depend on the oven):

- A 2 cm thick stone placed in a standard oven about 8 cm below the heating element heats up to 300–330°C (or more) in about 30–35 min (grill mode at the maximum temperature, usually 250–270°C).
- A 3 cm thick stone in the same oven needs about an hour.

Plan 45–60 min for heating the stone (a margin over the times in the examples, because ovens differ in power). The best indicator is a measurement with a pyrometer (an infrared thermometer), which helps you understand your own oven and stone. For more on heating the stone, see the question [How do I position and heat the stone in the oven?](#faq-stone-setup)

**What types of stones are there?**

- **Fireclay** – a very popular pizza stone, used in most ovens. It has a porous structure that draws moisture out of the dough very well. Above 400°C, however, home bakers’ observations suggest it can burn the bottoms of pizzas, so for baking at such temperatures consider replacing it with biscotto ([biscotto for the F1 oven](#faq-biscotto-f1)).
- **Cordierite** – often found as a 1 cm thick stone, and it comes as standard equipment with the Ooni Koda. Home bakers’ observations suggest that it copes with high temperatures a bit better than fireclay, but not as well as biscotto.
- **Biscotto** – a stone fired from clay, whose structure is suited to baking pizza at temperatures above 400°C without much risk of burning the bottom.
- **Granite** – you can bake pizza on it, but its structure is less porous, so it draws moisture out of the dough less well.
- **Heavily marketed stones** – Lava Stone, stones advertised as made from Vesuvius magma and similar. With these, marketing plays a big role: according to home bakers’ observations they often cost more than classic fireclay and match biscotto in price, even though they do not give similar results. They are usually not worth paying extra for, but if you already own one, in most cases it bakes at the level of fireclay, probably not better.
:::

::: q #faq-granite-slab Can I use an ordinary granite slab (for example a building-grade one) for baking?
Granite for construction, finishing or everyday use is sometimes polished or chemically impregnated to protect it from moisture and frost. Ask the seller or manufacturer how it was processed, because contact between food and such a stone can be dangerous to health. Granite can also crack with sudden changes in temperature, so heat it slowly, together with the oven.

Opinions are divided: some people use a cheap granite slab for about $4–5 and say it works very well, while others think it is better to pay extra for a stone intended for food contact (about $15–25). It is safer to buy fireclay (about $15–25): it is usually a better pizza stone and will serve you for a long time; granite intended for food contact is another option.
:::

::: q #faq-cracked-stone My pizza stone cracked. Is it still effective?
Stones do crack. The cause is often a sudden change in temperature (for example a cold stone put into a hot oven, or water poured onto a hot stone), how the stone is placed in the oven, or the fact that it is not perfectly flat, so it is exposed to constant surface stress during heating. A crack usually does not affect how well the stone works as long as it does not fall apart: it may look less attractive, but it bakes practically the same.
:::

::: q #faq-combo-method What is the “combo” method (frying pan and oven)?
The combo method lets you bake pizza in the oven without a pizza stone. The name comes from the combination of the oven and the frying pan. The pan and the heating element get very hot, so use oven gloves. The process looks like this:

1. Heat a flat frying pan to a high temperature (without oil). Use a steel or cast-iron pan. Do not use a non-stick pan or any pan with a non-stick coating: an empty pan heated hard, especially under the grill element, goes past the temperature at which such a coating starts to break down (about 260°C) and gives off harmful fumes. The flatter the pan, the better.
2. Shape the dough disc and lay it straight away on the heated pan.
3. While the dough disc is on the pan, add the toppings and watch it bake through from underneath. There are many variables, but the dough disc should stay on the pan for at least 2 min. Spots and browning should appear on the underside, which means it is baked through from underneath. You can gradually increase the temperature while watching the underside.
4. Then you have two options (in both, the bake should take 1.5–3 min, depending on the temperature, the distance from the heating element and the amount of toppings):
   - Put the pan into a preheated oven in grill mode, right under the heating element (the pan must not have a plastic or wooden handle).
   - Slide the dough disc from the pan onto a baking tray and put it into a preheated oven in grill mode, right under the heating element.
5. Remove the finished pizza.
:::

::: q #faq-clean-stone How do I clean a pizza stone?
**Do not clean a stone with chemicals.** A porous stone absorbs cleaning products. The best way is pyrolysis (ashing), which happens at a temperature above 400°C and turns all the dirt into ash. In an ordinary oven you can try to achieve this effect partly, by placing the stone on the highest shelf, right under the top heating element (without touching it), and setting the heating to maximum. After 30–40 min at full temperature (counted from the moment the oven has heated up), lighter dirt should disappear; this way the stone usually reaches about 300–330°C, which is less than in an oven with a pyrolytic function (about 500°C). If the marks do not disappear, repeat the process. A red-hot heating element is above 500°C (usually much more), which is why the stone right under it heats up the most.

If you cannot carry out pyrolysis, wash the stone with water alone and remove baked-on dirt with a spatula or a stiff bristle brush (not a wire one). Dry the stone thoroughly before heating it again, and never pour water on a hot stone.
:::

::: q #faq-pyrometer What is a pyrometer used for when baking pizza?
A pyrometer is used primarily to measure the approximate temperature of the stone in the oven. You can also use it for a very rough measurement of the dough temperature during kneading, but the reading is only indicative.

::: q #faq-new-stone Does a new stone need any preparation before the first bake?
The best way is to heat it up before first use just as you would before baking a pizza: put the stone into a cold oven and heat it gradually, so that dust and impurities burn off. Makers of biscotto stones may recommend a first heating in stages, so check the instructions for your stone. After this heating you can bake on it straight away or let it cool and use it next time. With a biscotto-type stone (especially Saputo Biscotto) you can also gently wipe it with a damp cloth before putting it into the oven (never wipe a hot stone) to remove the dust on the surface.
:::

::: q #faq-burnt-bottom The bottom of my pizza burns when baking. What could be the problem?
There can be several causes:

1. The stone heats up to over 400°C, so (according to home bakers’ observations) it releases its heat too quickly and burns the bottom. This problem is common with fireclay and cordierite stones (probably granite too). Replace the stone with a “biscotto” stone, which is used in ovens for baking Neapolitan pizza. Because of its construction and structure it releases heat differently, which makes it much harder to burn the bottom of a pizza on it (this happens too, but only at much higher stone temperatures).
2. Too much dusting flour ended up on the stone along with the dough disc, burned on the hot stone and left burnt specks on the underside of the dough disc.
:::

::: q #faq-biscotto-f1 Which biscotto should I buy for the F1 (Effeuno P134H)?
Two kinds of biscotto stone are used with the F1 oven (the descriptions below are based on users’ experience, and the heating times are approximate):

1. **Saputo Biscotto** – the most popular stone, 3 cm thick, used in professional ovens for Napoletana. Its great advantage is that it accumulates heat better and releases it more slowly, which makes it the best solution for this type of pizza, especially for baking many pizzas in a row. However, because of its thickness it is hard to bake other pizzas on it with good results if they need lower temperatures and longer baking times (Classica, Teglia alla Romana, Pala alla Romana, Pinsa Romana). The stone cannot maintain the right temperature on its surface for the whole bake, because of its high thermal inertia (the bottom heating element has half the power of the top one). This shows up as a poorly baked bottom. These kinds of pizza are better baked on the original fireclay that comes in the F1 set. Heating the Saputo Biscotto for baking Napoletana requires the oven to run for about 45–55 min.
2. **Fiesoli Arte Biscotto** – an alternative version of biscotto, made from different clay mixtures, with results similar to those of Saputo. For the F1 this stone is about 1.5 cm thick, which makes it more versatile, because you can bake all kinds of pizza on it. Because it is thinner, the bottom heating element can easily maintain the desired temperature on the surface of the stone during the bake. Be careful when baking Napoletana, though: turning both knobs on the oven up to the maximum will most likely burn the pizza, because the stone has less mass and accumulates high temperatures less well. To limit or even eliminate this problem, users lower the setting of the bottom heating element to 300–350°C (F1) or 350–400°C (F1 HA version). Some bakers also make a good Napoletana using only the top heating element. Heating the Fiesoli Arte Biscotto for baking Napoletana requires the oven to run for about 30–40 min.
:::

::: q #faq-rotate-f1 Do I need to turn the pizza during baking in the F1?
It depends on at least two factors:

1. How far into the oven you launch the dough disc. If it is launched too far in, the top heating element at the back of the oven does not heat the sides evenly. Turn the pizza and move it to the central part of the stone.
2. F1 ovens are not all equal. The classic versions, with the original thermostats, heat up to about 450°C, and the versions with a 500°C thermostat to about 500°C or more. Because of these differences the heat reaches the pizza slightly differently, and then it is worth turning it. In ovens with 500°C thermostats the problem is probably smaller.
:::

::: q #faq-stone-setup How do I position and heat the stone in the oven?
1. Place the stone on the highest shelf, so that there are about 7–12 cm between the stone and the top heating element. Put it on a baking tray (a regular or an upside-down one) or on a rack; on a rack more heat may escape to the unused lower part of the oven.
2. Turn on grill mode (the top heating element runs at boosted power) and set the maximum temperature. Check in the oven’s manual whether grill mode may be used with the door closed and for 45–60 min without a break. If the oven has no grill function, set top-and-bottom heating to the maximum temperature. Heat the stone from a cold oven for about 45–60 min (a stone 3 cm thick for about an hour). **Always take everything else out of the oven when baking pizza.**

**Why close to the heating element?** A red-hot heating element is above 500°C (usually much more). It cannot heat the whole oven to such a temperature, because the oven has too large a volume and loses heat too quickly.

:::

::: q #faq-oven-mods What are the risks of modifying home ovens to reach higher temperatures?
Home ovens are built for longer operation at the temperatures they were designed for. Forcing them to run hotter than the manufacturer intended will most likely cause problems with longer use, for example:

1. Melted oven control elements (knobs, dials)
2. Melted internal insulation of the oven glass or the oven door
3. Cracked oven glass from a sudden difference in temperature (opening and closing the door)
4. Damage to the oven’s control mechanisms (overheating, melted insulation)
5. Damage to the furniture next to the oven housing, for example peeling veneer or paint

::: warning Do not modify your oven
Modifying an oven (interfering with its thermostat, safety devices or heating elements) is risky: it involves very high temperatures and mains electricity, can cause a fire or damage the appliance, can void the warranty and is not recommended. An ordinary oven with a stone is enough for your first pizzas.
:::
:::

::: q #faq-grill Why are barbecues, barbecue attachments and ovens with only bottom heating usually not suitable for baking pizza?
At first glance a barbecue seems like a great tool for baking, because it reaches very high temperatures, but it is usually not suitable for pizza. Pizza needs heat from both the top and the bottom, and for Neapolitan pizza the temperature at the top is often even higher than at the bottom. Because of its construction, a typical barbecue generates heat mainly from the bottom, so pizzas baked on it are often hard and very crunchy on the bottom, but pale and underbaked on top. The temperature above and below the pizza has to be more even so that the dough disc bakes nicely.
:::

::: q #faq-gas-oven-home Can I bake pizza in a home gas oven?
Yes, if the oven has top heating. If not, the situation will be similar to the one with the [barbecue](#faq-grill). Do not cover the slots in the floor of a gas oven, or a whole rack, with foil, a stone or a tray: this blocks the air flow and can cause carbon monoxide poisoning. Put the stone on the top shelf and follow the oven’s instructions.
:::

::: q #faq-gas-pizza-ovens Can a gas pizza oven be used at home?
**Outdoors only.** Gas pizza ovens (for example the Ooni Koda) are designed for outdoor use only. The manufacturers’ instructions forbid using them in a building, a garage or any other enclosed space, and on or under a balcony; good ventilation of a room does not change this. Also check local regulations and building rules on using gas cylinders.

A gas oven needs a lot of fresh air as well as fuel. Without enough fresh air the gas burns incompletely and produces carbon monoxide: a colourless, odourless gas that can kill without warning. Place the oven in a well-ventilated spot outdoors, sheltered from strong wind. A carbon monoxide alarm in rooms the fumes could reach is an extra safeguard, not permission to use the oven indoors.

Use the gas and the regulator specified in the oven’s instructions. Before each use, check the connections (cylinder, regulator, hose) with soapy water: bubbles mean a leak, and then the oven must not be lit. Never look for a leak with a flame. After baking, close the cylinder valve. Propane vaporises at lower temperatures than butane (boiling point about −42°C against about −1°C), so an oven running on propane works more reliably in cold weather; the flame temperature of propane and butane is practically the same.

::: warning
Never leave a gas oven unattended while it is lit – you are working with an open flame.
:::
:::

::: q #faq-first-oven Which oven should I choose to start with?
A home oven with a grill mode and the option of setting top heating at a temperature of at least 250°C is entirely enough to start with. Home ovens differ: in some, grill mode can be set to 270°C, and a stone directly under the heating element then heats up to over 300°C (according to home bakers’ measurements). This is because a red-hot heating element is above 500°C (usually much more). The heating element cannot heat the whole oven to such a temperature, because the oven has too large a volume, but a stone placed close to it absorbs more heat and reaches a higher temperature that way.

If your oven reaches only about 220–230°C or has no grill mode, you can still start: heat the stone as described in [How do I position and heat the stone in the oven?](#faq-stone-setup). The bake will be longer than in the table of [baking times](#faq-bake-times), and the crust paler and less crisp.

If you want to invest in a separate pizza oven, popular models are described in the [overview of ovens](#ovens).
:::

::: q #faq-caliente-napoletana Can Napoletana be baked in home electric ovens such as the Spice Caliente or the G3 Ferrari Delizia?
In the factory version, usually not (for example the G3 Ferrari Delizia reaches up to 400°C and bakes a pizza in about 5 min, while Napoletana takes 60–90 s in an oven above 400°C), but these ovens are well suited to Classica-type pizza. For Napoletana you need an oven that reaches 450–500°C ([overview of ovens](#ovens)); modifying ovens is risky ([risks of modification](#faq-oven-mods)).
:::

::: q #faq-thermomix Can I knead pizza dough in a Thermomix or a similar multifunction kitchen machine?
“Thermomix”-type machines are multifunctional kitchen tools for home use. They also have a dough kneading option, but because of their construction and built-in blades, according to home bakers’ experience, they are not a good choice. During mixing the structure of the dough is constantly torn instead of being strengthened by stretching. The dough may also overheat faster, so after stronger stretching it tears more easily.

If you still want to use one, the best approach is to use the Thermomix for the initial kneading of the dough (2–3 min) and then finish kneading by hand. Home bakers have tested this in practice.
:::

::: q #faq-stand-mixer Which stand mixer should I buy to start with?
The comparison below is based on the opinions of home pizza makers and on which mixers stand out. The budgets given are approximate.

- **Budget of about $125** – at this price you can find dozens of different mixers, which often have the same mechanism in a different housing. The most common in this segment are Bosch mixers from the MUM series. In practice, mixers in this range cannot cope with large amounts of dough (over about 1 kg), even though the manufacturers claim more. Do not rely on the power or capacity figures manufacturers state.
- **Budget of about $250** – the G3 Ferrari G20075 stands out here. There are many others, probably just as good.
- **Budget of about $500** – two brands stand out here: KitchenAid (Artisan series) and Kenwood (Chef series). These mixers are built for kneading dough; the larger models can knead even 1.5–2 kg (and more) of dough at once, the smaller ones noticeably less. They are also good multifunctional tools for the home, but here what matters most is kneading dough.
- **Budget of over $750** (for professionals and experienced amateurs) – here you enter the group of professional mixers that you can use at home. The best of them are spiral mixers.
:::
