## Equipment {#equipment}

### Oven {#ovens}

**You do not need to buy an oven for your first pizza.** A home oven with a grill mode and a pizza stone is enough ([which oven is enough](#faq-first-oven)). The ovens below are for people who want to bake at a higher temperature than a home oven allows, for example Napoletana at 450–500°C.

**Budget under $250:**

- **Spice Caliente** – an electric countertop oven; in its factory version (maximum about 400°C) it bakes Classica-style pizza well, but it does not reach the temperatures needed for a true Napoletana.

::: warning Do not modify your oven
Modifying an oven (interfering with its thermostat, safety devices or heating elements) is risky: it involves very high temperatures and mains electricity, can cause a fire or damage the appliance, can void the warranty and is not recommended. An ordinary oven with a stone is enough for your first pizzas.
:::

**Budget over $250:**

The ovens below reach a temperature of 450–500°C and require no modifications.

- **Ooni Koda 12, Koda 16 and the newer Koda 2** – gas-fired (for outdoor use); the Koda 16 is larger, with an L-shaped burner and more room.
- **Ooni Karu 2** – a multi-fuel oven (wood or charcoal, optional gas burner), for outdoor use.
- **Ooni Fyra 12** (successor to the discontinued Ooni 3, formerly Uuni 3) – a portable wood-pellet oven, for outdoor use.
- **Effeuno P134H** (also called the “F1” in this guide) – an electric oven of about 3 kW (depending on the version), available in versions with a maximum temperature from about 450°C up to 509°C (depending on the model).

Gas, pellet and wood-fired ovens are for outdoor use only (more on gas ovens: [gas pizza ovens](#faq-gas-pizza-ovens)).

### Scale, containers, stone and peels {#accessories}

**Scale** – all amounts in this guide are by weight, because cups and spoons are too inexact for flour and yeast, so you need a kitchen scale and a jewellery scale for the yeast (about $5). Yeast is usually added in amounts from a fraction of a gram to a few grams, and ordinary kitchen scales are accurate to only 1 g, so the yeast needs a scale accurate to 0.01 g (some jewellery scales manage only 0.1 g). If you do not have one, see [a method without a jewellery scale](#faq-yeast-no-scale).

![Pocket jewellery scale with a display](img/scale.webp)

**Containers** – used for storing the dough, as a bulk dough or as dough balls, during maturation and rising. Containers of 40×30×7 cm with a lid (the grey one in the photo) work well. Choose a container with a smooth lid, without hinges or holes. Ordinary food containers work too (the second photo shows a tried-and-tested way of storing dough balls). If you plan to keep the bulk dough in the fridge, any food container that closes tightly is enough.

Look for a smooth bottom: with embossed patterns or an uneven surface it is harder to slide a spatula under a dough ball and lift it out of the container.

**Spatula** (putty-knife style; in English usually sold as a dough scraper or bench scraper) – makes it easier to divide the bulk dough and to lift the dough balls out of the container before baking. A simple one costs about $3–5 (models from well-known makers can cost several times more). Look for a stainless steel (inox) one, without paint or coatings, and wash it thoroughly before first use. Well-known makers of pizza equipment, for example Lilly Codroipo and Gi.Metal, also make spatulas.

![Stainless steel spatula with a wooden handle](img/spatula.webp)

**Pizza stone** – for people who bake in a ordinary oven. Most are made of cordierite or fireclay, and some of granite. At a similar thickness they give similar results in a home oven; they differ mainly in how well they withstand sudden temperature changes and in their porosity.

Stones come in a thin version (about 1 cm) and a thicker one (about 2–3 cm), round or rectangular. A rectangular stone is more convenient and more forgiving when you slide the pizza on unevenly, because a pizza slips off a round stone more easily. A thicker stone takes a long time to heat up (3 cm: about 1 h), but it holds the temperature longer, which is useful if you bake many pizzas in a row. For an oven, a good compromise is a thickness of about 1.5–2 cm: it heats up relatively quickly, and the temperature drop when you open the oven is smaller than with a thinner stone. For your first stone, choose a rectangular one (about 35×40 cm), 1.5–2 cm thick and intended for food contact ([how to choose a pizza stone](#faq-stone)).

**Pizza peel** – used to carry the pizza from the work surface to the oven and, if needed, to move it around in the oven. The choice is wide, from simple models to professional ones. To start with, plywood peels are enough. Without a peel, a rimless baking sheet (or a rimmed one turned upside down) dusted with semolina or flour does the same job. Well-known makers of professional peels include Lilly Codroipo and Gi.Metal.

**Turning peel** (optional) – for more experienced pizza lovers; it is used to turn the pizza conveniently in the oven. It is useful when the oven heats unevenly, for example more strongly from one side (typical of gas ovens, but it also happens in electric ones).
