## Your first pizza step by step {#first-bake}

**There is no single perfect dough recipe.** No recipe guarantees that every bake will turn out well and be repeatable. Success depends on many factors: air temperature and humidity, the type of flour (strength and fineness of the grind), maturation and rising times and – probably most importantly – your own skills. Do not be afraid to experiment, and write down what you change between bakes.

::: tip Good to know
**There is no such thing as instant pizza.** You can have a frozen pizza, a delivered pizza or a flatbread instantly, but dough for real Italian pizza needs time, and it pays you back with flavour. For low-strength flours the minimum maturation and rising time is 6–8 h in total, counted from the moment the dough is kneaded to the moment the pizza goes into the oven. Treat this as a minimum: a longer time usually improves the flavour and look of the baked pizza (as long as you do not overdo it – see the warning “Do not shorten the times” below). Pizza flours start at about W220 (that is still low strength; medium strength is W250–310).
:::

### Rising vs maturation {#rising-vs-maturation}

**Rising** is the increase in the volume of the dough: the yeast feeds on the sugars in the flour and produces carbon dioxide, which makes the dough puff up. **Maturation** covers the slower changes in the dough: enzymes break down the starch and proteins of the flour, so the dough gains flavour and aroma and stretches better.

Both processes happen at the same time, but at different speeds. In a warm place the yeast works quickly, so the dough can grow before it has had time to mature – that is why **dough that has risen is not always mature**. In the fridge (about 3–5°C) growth is very slow, while maturation carries on. Mature dough tastes better and is easier to shape.

::: tip Tip
To start with, choose a hydration of **55–58%**: this is the lower part of the range used in original Italian pizzerias (the AVPN rules allow about 55–62%). Try a higher hydration only once you have gained experience working with dough.
:::

### Basic recipe {#recipe}

The recipe is given in **baker’s percentages**: the weight of the flour is always 100%, and the other ingredients are a percentage of it. This makes it easy to scale the recipe to any number of pizzas. The example is a **Classica** pizza (diameter 30–33 cm) made from 4 dough balls of 240 g each – each ball makes one pizza, which you can bake even in an ordinary oven on a stone.

| Ingredient | Amount | % of flour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medium-strength flour (W250–310) | 598 g | 100% |
| Cold water | 329 g | 55% |
| Salt | 16 g | approx. 2.7% |
| Olive oil or other vegetable oil | 16 g | approx. 2.7% |
| Fresh yeast | 1.24 g | approx. 0.2% |
| **Total** | **960 g** | 4 dough balls of 240 g |

Salt is about 50 g per litre of water. Use cold tap water (as a rough guide: when the kitchen is above 25°C, use water chilled in the fridge). Weigh the yeast on a jewellery scale accurate to 0.01 g – ordinary kitchen scales (accurate to 1 g) cannot measure such a small amount. No such scale? Here is a [way to do it without a jewellery scale](#faq-yeast-no-scale).

**Dry yeast.** Instead of 1.24 g of fresh yeast use about 0.4 g of instant yeast or about 0.5 g of active dry yeast (approximate values, worked out with the [conversion](#faq-yeast-types)). Mix instant yeast into the flour before adding the salt (manufacturers advise avoiding direct contact between yeast and salt). Active dry yeast is usually dissolved first in a few spoonfuls of lukewarm water – the one exception to the cold-water rule – and you measure the rest of the 329 g of water cold.

**Schedule** (24 h in total, room temperature about 20°C): 2 h at room temperature (bulk dough) → 16 h in the fridge (bulk dough) → 2 h at room temperature (bulk dough) → 4 h at room temperature (dough balls) → bake.

The amount of yeast is matched to this schedule and to a temperature of about 20°C. The longer the dough rises and the warmer the kitchen is, the less yeast it needs, and vice versa. If you change the schedule, or your kitchen is much warmer, adjust the amount of yeast to match. Beyond that, change only one thing at a time (a new schedule and the yeast adjusted to it count as one change), so that you know what made the difference. Measure the temperature where the dough will stand – an ordinary room thermometer is enough. On hot days (26–28°C) the dough balls may be ready after about 3 h instead of 4–5 h, so start checking them earlier ([when a dough ball is ready](#faq-ball-ready)).

::: warning Do not shorten the times
Before going into the fridge, the dough usually needs at least 2 h at room temperature so that the yeast gets going. Once shaped, the dough balls usually need about 4–5 h at room temperature to regain their volume. Leaving the dough balls too long is harmful as well: after about 6 h (sooner in warm weather) they start to deflate and may spread out. If you need to bake a few hours later than planned, leave the bulk dough in the fridge for those hours. If the dough balls are ready before you are, close the container and put the balls in the fridge for a few hours; take them out about 1 hour before baking, so that they warm up a little.
:::

**How do you scale the recipe to a different number of pizzas?** Multiply the number of dough balls by the weight of one ball, then divide the result by (1 + water + salt + fat + yeast), entering the percentages as decimal fractions. In the example: 960 g ÷ (1 + 0.55 + 0.027 + 0.027 + 0.002) ≈ 598 g of flour. The other ingredients are the corresponding percentage of that weight. For two pizzas halve the amounts in the table (299 g flour, 165 g water, 8 g salt, 8 g oil and 0.6 g fresh yeast); for one pizza take a quarter (150 g, 82 g, 4 g, 4 g and 0.3 g). You will find the parameters of other pizza types (hydration, fat, dough ball weight, time) in the [Pizza types compared](#pizza-guides) section. If you use dry yeast, see the [conversion](#faq-yeast-types).

### Kneading the dough {#kneading}

Step by step:

1. Put all the flour into a large mixing bowl or the bowl of a stand mixer.
2. Measure out 329 g of cold water.
3. Dissolve 1.24 g of yeast in the water, rubbing it with your fingers.
4. Add 16 g of salt to the flour (in a stand mixer you can add the salt after a few minutes of kneading – it dissolves more easily).
5. Add the water with the yeast to the flour (in a stand mixer pour it in gradually).
6. Add 16 g of oil (in a stand mixer it is best towards the end of kneading – the bowl then comes cleaner of dough residue).
7. Knead the dough until it is smooth and elastic.
8. Shape the dough into one ball (the bulk dough), put it in a container with a tight lid, without oiling the dough, and close the lid. Then follow the schedule above: 2 h at room temperature, then the fridge.

**Kneading by hand.** First mix with your hand in the bowl until the ingredients come together. Then transfer the dough to the work surface and knead it, stretching and folding – push the dough away with the heel of your hand, fold it back over itself, give it a quarter turn and repeat – until it is smooth and elastic (usually about 10–15 minutes; judge by the dough, not by the clock). Do not add extra flour – if the dough sticks, wet your hands with water.

**Stand mixer.** Knead on low speed (usually about 5–10 minutes) until the dough is smooth and starts to come away from the sides of the bowl; judge by the dough, not by the clock. Hand mixers with dough hooks are usually limited to about 500 g of flour with the other ingredients (check the manual), which is less than this recipe needs, so for this amount knead by hand.

**When is the dough ready?** It is smooth and elastic, and a small piece pulled gently apart between your fingertips stretches into a thin membrane before it tears. With a long maturation, as in this recipe, the membrane does not have to be perfectly translucent – the gluten keeps developing during maturation, while kneading for too long only warms the dough and weakens it. Also watch the temperature: the dough being kneaded should not stay above about 25–26°C for long (see [dough temperature during kneading](#faq-dough-temperature)). That is why you use cold water and, when kneading in a mixer, take breaks if the dough gets warm.

::: warning Do not taste raw dough
Flour is a raw product and can carry harmful bacteria, which only baking kills. Do not eat or taste raw flour or raw dough, and after handling it wash your hands, bowls, utensils and work surface with warm soapy water.
:::

### A few important rules {#dough-tips}

- Before going into the fridge, the yeast in the dough must get going. It usually needs at least 2 h at room temperature (longer, depending on your schedule).
- While the dough is rising, do not dust the bulk dough or the dough balls with flour and do not oil them (you use dusting flour only when shaping the dough disc).
- After taking the dough out of the fridge, wait about 1–2 h until it warms up a little. Only then shape the dough balls. Shaped dough balls need about 4–5 h before baking (less in hot weather), so that you can shape the pizza from them without any problems.
- Do not roll the dough balls with a rolling pin, because it pushes out the air that formed during maturation and rising.
- **Always** store the bulk dough and the dough balls in a tightly closed container, and take the dough out only for dividing it into balls or for baking.
