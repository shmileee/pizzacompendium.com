### Dough: kneading, rising and baking {#faq-dough}

::: q #faq-fat When should I add oil or olive oil (fat), and what does it change in the bake?
Fat makes pizza more tender and is a typical ingredient of pizzas baked in a style other than Neapolitan (Neapolitan dough made to AVPN rules contains no fat), such as Classica, Teglia alla Romana, Pala alla Romana or – often, though not always – Pinsa Romana. You can use ordinary vegetable oil, olive oil or even lard; the result is always a more tender dough. Fat also limits the bonds that form in the gluten network, so the dough is less springy and stretches more easily.

**When to add fat during kneading.** In a mixer, add it towards the end of kneading, when the dough already forms a uniform mass; it helps gather the rest of the ingredients from the bowl. When kneading by hand, mix it with the water or add it separately when combining the ingredients (lumps of oil can form in the flour).
:::

::: q #faq-ball-ready How can I tell that a dough ball is ready for baking?
A ready dough ball is clearly bigger, soft and airy, but it does not spread out. Press it gently with a finger: if the dent springs back slowly, the ball is ready; if it springs back at once, it needs more time; if the dent stays and the ball sinks, it has over-risen.

The average weight of the dough balls was about 260 g, and the average flour strength was about W300 (a rough estimate).

In this case the dough balls may have risen faster, because the amount of yeast was increased by about 40% relative to the calculated amount, and the room temperature was 24°C. The photo after 4 h was taken right before the first bake.

Dough balls shaped from a bulk dough that has not yet gained volume may be smaller after shaping than in the photo, so more time may be needed. In this example the bulk dough had already gained volume, and there was a lot of air in it when it was portioned. At a lower temperature the time is also longer, and with a higher hydration the shape of the dough ball may be different. In a cooler room (below about 20°C) the dough balls may need up to about 5–6 h.

::: q #faq-dough-temperature Does the temperature of the dough during kneading matter?
It is generally accepted that the dough being kneaded should not stay above about 25–26°C for long (at a low hydration, below 60%, some bakers accept even about 27–28°C). If the dough exceeds this temperature over a longer kneading time, the gluten network may start to weaken, which shows up as the dough tearing easily or being sticky. If the dough slightly exceeds this threshold at the end of kneading, no harm is done and there is no need to worry.

Overheating the dough also makes the yeast work faster and more intensively, which throws off the planned rising process.
:::

::: q #faq-dry-dough What should I do if the dough dries out?
Dough drying out is most often a sign that its container is not airtight enough. Sometimes it is enough to cover the dough with cling film or put the container into a larger food container. Air can get in through hinges or holes in the lid, so choose a container with a smooth, tightly fitting lid.

Dough also dries out in the fridge if the container is not airtight, so close it tightly while it is stored in the fridge as well.
:::

::: q #faq-why-fridge Why is the fridge used for rising and maturing dough?
In the fridge (about 3–5°C) the growth of the dough is greatly slowed, while maturation continues, although slowly. Mature dough has a better flavour and is easier to shape. The lower the temperature, the more slowly the yeast works and the slower all the processes in the dough are.

The fridge mostly helps you plan the bake. For example, you have time on Thursday evening to make the dough, but it has to last until Saturday at 16:00. The fridge works very well here, because with a typical amount of yeast no flour lasts that long at room temperature (the structure of such dough would be very weak). This assumes a room temperature of around 20–23°C – dough rises and matures differently at 12°C than at 20–23°C.
:::

::: q #faq-room-vs-fridge What is the difference between rising dough at room temperature and in the fridge, and what are the benefits of a bulk dough?
**Room temperature (about 20–23°C).** The yeast works quickly, so the dough rises and matures in roughly a dozen hours. Good flours (W270–310) usually last up to about 16 h in such conditions (an approximate figure, depending on the amount of yeast and the temperature; see [flour strength](#flour-strength)). In summer, when it is 26–28°C at home, the process speeds up and the dough easily over-rises.

**The fridge (about 3–5°C).** The yeast works very slowly, so the dough hardly rises, but it matures. This lets you spread the process over 24–48 h, or even longer. Before the fridge the dough usually needs about 2 h at room temperature so that the yeast gets going. After you take it out, it needs about 1–2 h to warm up and another 4–5 h as dough balls to regain volume (approximate figures).

**Bulk dough or dough balls?** A bulk dough (one large ball) works well for long maturation, including in the fridge. Shape the dough balls only a few hours before baking. Dough balls shaped straight after kneading work for short rising and low hydration – see [dough balls or bulk dough](#faq-balls-vs-block).
:::

::: q #faq-sifting Do I need to sift the flour?
No. Home bakers’ experience shows that sifting has no real effect on the mixing of the ingredients or on the final dough.
:::

::: q #faq-room-temp-only Can I make the dough entirely at room temperature, skipping the fridge?
Yes. Rising and maturation entirely at room temperature (about 20–23°C) works with flours of strength W270–310. For example, dough made with Polselli Vivace flour (W290), which the manufacturer recommends for long maturation, lasts that way for up to about 16 h, according to home bakers’ experience. If you plan 16 h, do 11 h as bulk dough and 5 h as dough balls (at 20–22°C).

The amount of yeast in the basic recipe is matched to a schedule with 16 h in the fridge. If the dough is to rise entirely at room temperature, use less yeast than in the recipe, check the dough more often and change only one thing at a time ([basic recipe](#recipe)).

With general-purpose flours that are not intended for pizza, take more care: judge their strength by the protein content (the more protein, the stronger the flour usually is).

Be especially careful in summer, when the temperature at home is often 26–28°C instead of 20–23°C. If you do not account for this, the yeast speeds up the planned process considerably, and the dough balls may spread out or over-rise (they will be ready sooner than planned, for example after 3 h instead of 5).
:::

::: q #faq-storing-dough How do I store dough during maturation and rising?
Store dough at every stage (whether as dough balls or as bulk dough) in a tightly closed container to prevent it from drying out. Make sure the shape of the container does not deform the dough balls, because taking them out and shaping them later becomes more difficult (a round dough ball is easier to stretch into a round pizza base). For the same reason the container should have a flat, smooth bottom.

You can also store dough balls in individual food containers. This makes better use of the space in the fridge, and you do not have to take all the dough balls out at once. A handy method is to place the dough balls on the lid of the box and close the box over them from above (so the box is upside down), which makes taking the dough balls out easier.
:::

::: q #faq-balling-after-fridge How long after taking the dough out of the fridge should I shape the dough balls?
It is usually worth letting the dough warm up after you take it out of the fridge (by convention this applies to doughs with a hydration lower than 68–70%). Dough with less water is stiffer and needs more time to become workable after the fridge. Shaping dough balls from cold dough can undo some of the yeast’s work in the fridge by squeezing out the “air” (CO₂), so the dough balls need more time to regain their volume.

It is generally accepted that an hour at room temperature is the minimum for the dough to warm up a little before you start shaping the dough balls. This is a generalisation: dough with 55% hydration probably needs an hour more than dough with about 60%. These suggestions are based on experience rather than on any rigid guidelines.

With high-hydration doughs (Canotto-style Napoletana, Teglia alla Romana, Pinsa Romana and similar) you can shape the dough balls straight after the fridge. Their structure is delicate, so cold dough is easier to work with, and there is less risk of squeezing the air out of the dough balls.
:::

::: q #faq-autolyse What is autolyse?
Autolyse is **a short rest of the flour with water alone**, without salt and yeast, before the actual kneading of the dough. The French baking technologist Raymond Calvel described the method in the 1970s.

**How to do it:** mix the flour and water only until combined, cover and leave, usually for 20–60 minutes. Then add the yeast and salt and knead as usual.

**What it does:** the flour absorbs water better, the gluten network starts to form, the dough is more extensible and needs less kneading. However, too long an autolyse can weaken the structure of the dough. For pizza it is optional – you can bake great pizza without it.
:::

::: q #faq-balls-vs-block Dough balls or bulk dough: what is the difference in storage?
Dough balls suit short rising times and a relatively low hydration (below 60%). With a longer time or a higher hydration (either one is enough), the dough balls are more likely to lose their shape and spread out.

A bulk dough works very well for a long maturation in the fridge or at room temperature. Shape the dough balls only a few hours before baking: 4–5 h at room temperature (an estimate, depending on the dough hydration and the room temperature).
:::

::: q #faq-biga-poolish What are biga and poolish?
Biga and poolish are both preferments used for making dough. Biga usually has a hydration of about 45–60% (depending on the recipe), and poolish most often 100% (equal amounts of flour and water). They give the dough a deeper flavour and aroma that are hard to get in single-stage processes. Biga usually gives a springier, stronger dough, and poolish a more extensible one. These are methods for more advanced bakers – to start with, single-stage dough is enough.
:::

::: q #faq-white-sauce How do I make white sauce?
Italian pizza usually has no separate white sauce: instead of tomato sauce, good-quality mozzarella is most often used. Sometimes the base is made from other cheeses, such as ricotta or mascarpone. Crème fraîche is also quite popular.

White sauces based on cream, gorgonzola and herbs are not a classic part of Italian pizza. Italy does, however, have pizze bianche, that is pizzas without tomato, for example with mozzarella and gorgonzola (not to be confused with the Roman “pizza bianca”, a plain flatbread with olive oil and salt).
:::

::: q #faq-hydration-flour What hydration can a given flour handle?
Not every flour can hold every hydration in pizza dough (this differs somewhat from bread dough). To find out what hydration a given flour can “carry”:

1. **First step:** check the flour’s strength and protein content. The higher the strength and the more protein, the more water the dough absorbs. The [section on flour strength](#flour-strength) will help.
2. **Second step:** check the [list of flours](#popular-flours), which gives a proven hydration for many flours, or the manufacturer’s recommendation on the packaging. Start with a hydration of 55–58% and increase it gradually, bake by bake.
:::

::: q #faq-tastiest-flour Which flour is the tastiest?
**The one that tastes best to you.**

There are no rankings, because taste is individual. Some people love Caputo and 5 Stagioni, while others find them tasteless, and the same goes for other flours. You will find descriptions of many flours in the [list of flours](#popular-flours).
:::

::: q #faq-leopard What is leopard spotting on the edges of Napoletana and what affects it?
Leopard spotting means the dark spots on the edges of Neapolitan pizza. It is most often the result of well-matured dough combined with an even distribution of the oven’s heat onto the edges of the pizza. Good leopard spotting is easier to achieve in live-fire ovens, but it is also possible in electric ovens (an example in the photo below, baked in the Effeuno P134H).

::: q #faq-bake-times How long does each pizza type take to bake?
The values below are averages – treat them as suggestions for home baking, not professional baking.

| Type of pizza | Temperature | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Napoletana | above 400°C, usually 450–500°C (wood-fired, gas or electric oven) | 60–90 s |
| Classica | about 300–350°C (wood-fired, gas or electric oven); at lower temperatures the bake has to be longer | 120–180 s |
| Home oven with the stone under the grill (grill mode) | usually 250–270°C on the thermostat; a stone under the element gets hotter (see [choosing a stone](#faq-stone) and [heating the stone](#faq-stone-setup)) | about 2–5 min, up to about 7 min in a weaker oven |
| Teglia alla Romana (in a pan) | about 300°C (in a home oven about 250–270°C) | about 8–10 min in an electric oven, about 12–15 min in a home oven |

Do not rely on the time alone; watch the pizza: a dough disc with fewer toppings bakes differently from one with more, and one oven will bake a pizza in 4–5 min while another does it in 2 min.
:::
